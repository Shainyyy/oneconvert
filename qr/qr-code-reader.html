Reading the code before it reads you

A QR code is unreadable to a human, which is exactly what makes it useful for sticking a long URL on a poster and exactly what makes it a phishing tool. A sticker placed over a legitimate code on a parking meter or a restaurant table sends people to a payment page they had no way of inspecting. Decoding to text first removes the guesswork.

What to look at once you can see it: the domain, and specifically the part immediately before the first single slash. paypal.com.secure-billing.net is not PayPal — the real domain is secure-billing.net. Shorteners hide this entirely, so a shortened link in a code on a physical surface deserves more suspicion than one in an email.

The same applies to the codes you did not choose to scan. Codes in unsolicited post, on flyers under a windscreen wiper, or stuck to public equipment are the ones worth checking here rather than with a camera that offers to open the link the moment it recognises it.

Why a code sometimes will not decode

The decoder needs the code to occupy a reasonable share of the image and to be roughly square-on. A photograph of a poster taken from across a room, where the code is 40 pixels wide, has no detail left to read. Cropping to the code before uploading fixes most failures.

Angle and curvature are the next cause. Codes have three large finder squares in the corners precisely so software can correct for perspective, but there are limits — a code on a curved bottle or photographed at a sharp angle distorts past what the correction handles. Blur is unrecoverable in a way that darkness is not, so a dim but sharp photo usually beats a bright blurry one.

Damage is the case error correction was designed for. Depending on the level chosen when the code was made, between 7 and 30 per cent of it can be missing and still decode, which is how codes survive a logo in the middle or a crease through a corner. Beyond that threshold there is nothing to recover, and no reader will do better than another.
